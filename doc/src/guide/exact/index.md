Ball arithmetic ([](guide/ball-arithmetic)) computes with
approximations and rigorous error bounds. This is enough to prove that
two numbers differ, by computing them accurately enough that their balls
are disjoint, but never to prove that two numbers are equal: a ball that
contains zero is compatible with a nonzero number that is very small.
Many computations need equality: is a pivot of a matrix zero, is a
leading coefficient zero, does a sequence of operations reproduce a
known constant? FLINT's exact real and complex numbers answer such
questions by representing numbers symbolically, so that arithmetic is
exact, numerical values are available to any precision, and equalities
and inequalities are proved. This part of the library was developed as
the separate library Calcium [@Joh2021calcium] and merged into FLINT in
version 3.0.

## What FLINT offers {#types}

- **Algebraic numbers**, [](qqbar): a `qqbar_t` is an algebraic number
  stored canonically by its minimal polynomial and an isolating ball. It
  needs no context, and every predicate (equality, sign, comparison,
  realness) is decided. Operations compute minimal polynomials, whose
  degrees can grow quickly, so it suits individual numbers of moderate
  degree. See [](qqbar/using).
- **Exact real and complex numbers**, [](ca): a `ca_t` is an element of
  a field $\mathbb{Q}(a_1, \ldots, a_n)$ generated by algebraic numbers
  and transcendental numbers such as $\pi$, $e^{\sqrt 2}$ or $\log 3$,
  constructed automatically as numbers are combined. It can also hold
  special values such as infinities and *Undefined*. Predicates return
  `T_TRUE`, `T_FALSE` or `T_UNKNOWN` (see [](calcium#truth)): equality
  of algebraic numbers is decided within configurable limits, and
  equality involving transcendental numbers in many but not all cases.
  Vectors, polynomials and matrices are provided by [](ca_vec),
  [](ca_poly) and [](ca_mat). See [](ca/using).
- **Symbolic expressions**, [](fexpr): an `fexpr_t` is an expression
  tree such as `Add(Sqrt(2), Pi)`, used to construct numbers from
  formulas and to print them as text or LaTeX; it is not a number type.
  See [](fexpr/using).
- **Generic rings**: the contexts `gr_ctx_init_real_qqbar`,
  `gr_ctx_init_complex_qqbar`, `gr_ctx_init_real_ca`,
  `gr_ctx_init_complex_ca` and related ones make these numbers available
  to the generic polynomial and matrix algorithms of [](gr) (see
  [](gr_domains#algebraic) and [](gr_domains#real-complex)).

For numbers known to lie in $\mathbb{Q}$ or in one fixed number field,
[](fmpq) and [](nf_elem) are much faster. [](algorithms/exact/overview) compares the four representations in
detail.

## When to use exact numbers {#when}

- **Instead of floating-point or ball arithmetic.** Exact numbers are
  far too slow to replace floating-point numbers in scientific
  computing. The main advantage of `ca_t` over [](arb) and [](acb) is
  exact comparison, and the precision is managed automatically. If a
  computation is mostly numerical, it is better to use ball arithmetic
  first and to fall back on exact numbers only where an exact comparison
  is needed. Exact numbers are also a tool to test numerical programs.
- **For algebraic numbers.** Use [](qqbar) when exact answers to all
  predicates are required and degrees stay moderate. Computations whose
  intermediate degrees explode, such as sums of many square roots, can
  be cheaper with [](ca), which keeps them in a field with several
  generators.
- **With transcendental numbers.** Use [](ca), and handle `T_UNKNOWN`:
  whether equality of such numbers is decidable is an open problem, and
  the implemented methods succeed in many but not all cases. What can be
  decided is explained in [](algorithms/exact/zero-testing#in-practice),
  and what to do when a test fails in [](ca/check-is-zero#unknown).
- **In computer algebra.** Computing with constants is a small but
  difficult part of what a system like Mathematica or SymPy does; the
  common problems are wrong numerical error bounds or branch cuts, and
  slowness when expressions grow. `ca_t` addresses them with rigorous
  numerical evaluation and fast polynomial arithmetic. It complements
  rather than replaces a general simplifier, which may use it as one of
  its tools.

Worked examples are in [](tutorials/exact), and the algorithms
(representations, arithmetic, zero testing, simplification, linear
algebra) in [](algorithms/exact).
