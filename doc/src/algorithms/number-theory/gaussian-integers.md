The ring $\Z[i]$ of Gaussian integers is a Euclidean domain with respect
to the norm $N(a + bi) = a^2 + b^2$, and hence a unique factorisation
domain. Its units are $\pm 1, \pm i$, and $1 + i$ is the unique prime
above 2, with $2 = -i(1 + i)^2$. Standard references are [@Coh1996,
§1.2] for Euclidean rings and [@Wei2000] for the binary algorithm.

## Division with remainder {#division}

::: proposition {#prop-division}
For $x, y \in \Z[i]$ with $y \ne 0$, let $q = \lfloor \operatorname{re}
(x/y) + 1/2 \rfloor + i \lfloor \operatorname{im}(x/y) + 1/2 \rfloor$
and $r = x - qy$. Then $N(r) \le N(y)/2$.
:::

::: proof
Each of the real and imaginary parts of $x/y - q$ lies in
$(-1/2, 1/2]$, so $|x/y - q|^2 \le 1/2$, and $N(r) = N(y)\,|x/y - q|^2$.
:::

`fmpzi_divrem` computes this quotient exactly: with
$t = 2x\bar y + N(y)(1 + i)$, it takes the floor quotients of the real
and imaginary parts of $t$ by $2N(y)$, which costs one complex
multiplication and two divisions. Ties are rounded upwards in each
coordinate, which makes the remainder unique. When $x$ has at least
three bits fewer than $y$ (in the larger of its parts), $|x/y| < 1/2$
and the quotient is zero without computation.

`fmpzi_divrem_approx` only guarantees $N(r) < N(y)$, which is all the
Euclidean algorithm needs. When the bit sizes of $x$ and $y$ differ by
less than 45, it computes the quotient from double-precision
approximations of $x$ and $y$ (scaled by a common power of 2 if they
are large). Each coordinate of the computed quotient then differs from
the corresponding coordinate of $x/y$ by at most $1/2 + \varepsilon$ with
a tiny $\varepsilon$, so $|x/y - q|^2 \le 2(1/2 + \varepsilon)^2 < 1$.
Otherwise it calls `fmpzi_divrem`.

## Canonical associates {#canonical}

Every nonzero $x$ has exactly one associate $i^k x$ with real part
positive and imaginary part in $(-\operatorname{re}, \operatorname{re}]$:
the four associates are the rotations of $x$ by multiples of $90°$, and
exactly one of them lies in the sector $-\pi/4 < \arg z \le \pi/4$.
`fmpzi_canonical_unit_i_pow` returns the exponent $k \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$
from two comparisons, and `fmpzi_canonicalise_unit` applies it. The GCD
functions return the canonical associate, so that the GCD is unique; for
example $\gcd(2, 1 + i) = 1 + i$, while $1 - i$, $-1 + i$ and $-1 - i$
are its other associates.

## Greatest common divisors {#gcd}

### Euclidean algorithm {#euclidean}

By [](#prop-division) each step of $(x, y) \gets (y, x \bmod y)$ at
least halves the norm, so for inputs of $n$ bits there are at most about
$2n$ steps, and the total cost is $O(n^2)$ bit operations as for
integers. `fmpzi_gcd_euclidean` uses `fmpzi_divrem`.
`fmpzi_gcd_euclidean_improved` uses `fmpzi_divrem_approx`, and as soon
as all four parts are below $2^{50}$ in absolute value it finishes in
double-precision arithmetic: the approximate quotient may differ from
the rounded one, but the remainder $x - qy$ is computed exactly in
doubles because all intermediate values stay below $2^{53}$, and any
quotient with $N(r) < N(y)$ gives a correct Euclidean sequence.

### The (1+i)-ary algorithm {#binary}

Weilert's analogue of the binary GCD [@Wei2000] replaces divisions by
additions and divisions by $1 + i$. Since $\Z[i]/(1 + i) \cong
\mathbf{F}_2$, any two elements $x, y$ not divisible by $1 + i$ satisfy
$x \equiv y \equiv 1$, so all four of $x \pm y$ and $x \pm iy$ are
divisible by $1 + i$. `fmpzi_gcd_binary` first removes the factors
$1 + i$ from both inputs, remembering the smaller multiplicity, and then
repeatedly replaces the larger of $x, y$ (by norm) by the one of
$x \pm y$, $x \pm iy$ of smallest norm, divided by the largest power of
$1 + i$. The four norms sum to $4(N(x) + N(y))$, so the smallest is at
most $N(x) + N(y) \le 2N(x)$, and the division by $1 + i$ at least
halves it; Weilert shows that the algorithm has quadratic bit
complexity, like the binary GCD for integers [@Wei2000]. The implementation estimates the norms in
double precision and falls back to `fmpzi_gcd_euclidean` when an input
has more than 500 bits.

### The lattice algorithm {#lattice}

The ideal $(x, y) = g\Z[i]$ generated by $x$ and $y$ is a lattice in
$\Z^2$, generated over $\Z$ by $x, ix, y, iy$ (as vectors of real and
imaginary parts). Its Hermite normal form is
$\begin{pmatrix} c & b \\ 0 & a \end{pmatrix}$ with $ac = N(g)$, and $g$
is, up to a unit, a shortest nonzero vector of the lattice: every
element is $gw$ with $N(w) \ge 1$. `fmpzi_gcd_shortest`, due to
D. Schultz, computes the Hermite form by integer GCDs of the coordinates
and then the shortest vector of the two-dimensional lattice in the
$\ell^\infty$ norm from the continued fraction expansion of an interval
around $a/b$, computed by the quasi-linear ball continued fraction
routine `_fmpq_ball_get_cfrac` of `fmpq`. A special case handles purely real or purely imaginary $g$,
where a vector $g(1 + i)$ has the same $\ell^\infty$ norm as $g$.

### Choice of algorithm {#choice}

`fmpzi_gcd` returns the canonical associate of the other input if one is
zero. If all parts are below $2^{50}$ in absolute value it uses the
double-precision Euclidean algorithm directly. If either input has both
parts of one word, or either input has at most 30000 bits, it uses
`fmpzi_gcd_euclidean_improved`. Otherwise it uses
`fmpzi_gcd_shortest`, first reducing the larger input modulo the smaller
with one approximate division if their sizes differ by more than a
factor 1.5.

## The prime 1 + i {#one-plus-i}

For $x = a + bi \ne 0$, let $s = \min(\operatorname{ord}_2 a,
\operatorname{ord}_2 b)$ (with $\operatorname{ord}_2 0 = \infty$). Then
$x = 2^s x'$ with $x'$ not divisible by 2, and $x'$ is divisible by
$1 + i$, exactly once, if and only if both parts of $x'$ are odd. Since
$2^s = (1 + i)^{2s} i^{-s}$, the multiplicity of $1 + i$ in $x$ is $2s$
or $2s + 1$. `fmpzi_remove_one_plus_i` computes it from the 2-adic
valuations of the parts, divides by $2^s$ with shifts, multiplies by
$i^{s}$, and divides by $1 + i$ once more using
$(a + bi)/(1 + i) = ((a + b) + (b - a)i)/2$ if needed.

## Primality {#primality}

The Gaussian primes are, up to units, $1 + i$, the rational primes
$p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, and the elements $\pi$ with $N(\pi) = p$ a
rational prime $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ [@Coh1996, §1.2]. Hence $a + bi$ is
prime if and only if either $ab = 0$ and the nonzero part is $\pm p$
with $p$ prime and $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, or $ab \ne 0$ and $a^2 + b^2$
is prime. `fmpzi_is_prime` applies this criterion with `fmpz_is_prime`,
which proves primality, and `fmpzi_is_probabprime` with
`fmpz_is_probabprime`. FLINT does not provide factorisation in
$\Z[i]$, which reduces to factoring the norm and, for each prime
$p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ dividing it, finding $\pi = \gcd(p, t + i)$ with
$t^2 \equiv -1 \pmod p$.

## Use in FLINT {#use}

- Division: `fmpzi_divrem`, `fmpzi_divrem_approx`, `fmpzi_divexact`.
- Normalisation: `fmpzi_canonical_unit_i_pow`, `fmpzi_canonicalise_unit`.
- GCD: `fmpzi_gcd_euclidean`, `fmpzi_gcd_euclidean_improved`,
  `fmpzi_gcd_binary`, `fmpzi_gcd_shortest`, `fmpzi_gcd`; the last is the
  GCD of the generic ring `gr_ctx_init_fmpzi`.
- `fmpzi_remove_one_plus_i`, `fmpzi_is_prime`, `fmpzi_is_probabprime`.
- Multiplication: `fmpzi_mul` and `fmpzi_sqr` use the complex
  multiplication `flint_mpn_mul_complex` when all parts are multiword;
  polynomial products use `_fmpzi_poly_mulmid`.
