A *lattice* is a discrete subgroup $L = \Z b_1 + \cdots + \Z b_d$ of
$\R^n$ generated by linearly independent vectors $b_1, \ldots, b_d$, its
*basis*. Lattice reduction transforms a basis into one whose vectors are
short and nearly orthogonal. The LLL algorithm of Lenstra, Lenstra and
Lovász [@LLL1982] does this in polynomial time, and its output is good
enough for factoring polynomials over $\Q$ [@LLL1982; @vHo2002], finding
integer relations and minimal polynomials of algebraic numbers,
simultaneous Diophantine approximation, and many problems in
cryptanalysis. FLINT implements LLL for integer lattices, given by a
basis (the rows of an `fmpz_mat_t`) or by a Gram matrix. There is no
generic `gr_mat` version of the reduction itself; `gr_mat` provides the
reducedness test `gr_mat_is_row_lll_reduced_naive`, which
`fmpz_mat_is_reduced` uses.

## LLL-reduced bases {#reduced}

Let $b_1^*, \ldots, b_d^*$ be the Gram–Schmidt orthogonalisation of the
basis: $b_i^* = b_i - \sum_{j < i} \mu_{ij} b_j^*$ with
$\mu_{ij} = \langle b_i, b_j^* \rangle / r_{jj}$ and
$r_{jj} = \lVert b_j^* \rVert^2$. Write
$r_{ij} = \mu_{ij} r_{jj} = \langle b_i, b_j^* \rangle$.

::: definition {#def-lll}
Let $\frac14 < \delta < 1$ and $\frac12 \le \eta < \sqrt{\delta}$. The
basis is *$(\delta, \eta)$-LLL-reduced* if
1. it is *size-reduced*: $|\mu_{ij}| \le \eta$ for all $j < i$, and
2. it satisfies *Lovász' condition*:
   $(\delta - \mu_{i,i-1}^2) \, r_{i-1,i-1} \le r_{ii}$ for $1 < i \le d$.
:::

The original definition has $\eta = \frac12$; the relaxation $\eta > \frac12$
is needed when the Gram–Schmidt data are only known approximately
[@NS2009]. FLINT's default parameters are $\delta = 0.99$ and
$\eta = 0.51$.

::: theorem {#thm-lll-short}
Let $\alpha = 1/(\delta - \eta^2)$. A $(\delta, \eta)$-LLL-reduced basis
satisfies $r_{ii} \ge r_{11} \alpha^{-(i-1)}$ and
$$
\lVert b_1 \rVert \le \alpha^{(d-1)/4} (\det L)^{1/d}, \qquad
\lVert b_1 \rVert \le \alpha^{(d-1)/2} \lambda_1(L),
$$
where $\lambda_1(L)$ is the length of a shortest nonzero vector of $L$.
:::

::: proof
Lovász' condition and $|\mu_{i,i-1}| \le \eta$ give
$r_{ii} \ge (\delta - \eta^2) r_{i-1,i-1} = r_{i-1,i-1}/\alpha$, hence the
first claim. Since $\det L = \prod_i r_{ii}^{1/2}$, the product of these
inequalities gives the first bound. For the second, a shortest vector
$v = \sum_i x_i b_i$ with largest index $k$ of a nonzero $x_i$ satisfies
$\lVert v \rVert^2 \ge x_k^2 r_{kk} \ge r_{kk} \ge
\alpha^{-(k-1)} r_{11}$.
:::

## The LLL algorithm {#lll}

::: algorithm LLL {#alg-lll}
**Input:** a basis $b_1, \ldots, b_d$; parameters $\delta$, $\eta$.

**Output:** a $(\delta, \eta)$-reduced basis of the same lattice.

1. $k \gets 2$.
2. While $k \le d$:
   1. *Size reduction*: for $j = k - 1, \ldots, 1$, if $|\mu_{kj}| > \eta$
      then $b_k \gets b_k - \lfloor \mu_{kj} \rceil b_j$ and update the
      $\mu_{kl}$, $l \le j$.
   2. If Lovász' condition holds at $k$, $k \gets k + 1$; otherwise swap
      $b_{k-1}$ and $b_k$, update the Gram–Schmidt data, and
      $k \gets \max(k - 1, 2)$.
:::

::: theorem {#thm-lll-terminates}
For an integer basis with $\lVert b_i \rVert \le B$, [](#alg-lll)
performs $O(d^2 \log B)$ swaps.
:::

::: proof
The potential $\Pi = \prod_{i=1}^{d} \det L_i^2$, where $L_i$ is the
lattice generated by $b_1, \ldots, b_i$, is a positive integer at most
$B^{d(d+1)}$. Size reduction does not change it, and a swap at $k$
multiplies $\det L_{k-1}^2$ by
$(r_{kk} + \mu_{k,k-1}^2 r_{k-1,k-1}) / r_{k-1,k-1} < \delta$ and leaves
the others unchanged. So there are at most
$\log_{1/\delta} B^{d(d+1)} = O(d^2 \log B)$ swaps.
:::

With exact rational arithmetic, as in `fmpz_mat_lll_original`, the
Gram–Schmidt data have $O(d \log B)$ bits and the algorithm costs
$O(d^5 n \log^3 B)$ bit operations with classical arithmetic
[@LLL1982]. `fmpz_mat_lll_storjohann` follows Storjohann's modified LLL
[@Sto1996]. It works with the integral quantities $D_j \mu_{ij}$ and
$D_j$, where $D_j = \prod_{l \le j} r_{ll}$ are the leading principal
minors of the Gram matrix, all obtained by fraction-free elimination of
the Gram matrix ([](algorithms/linear-algebra/fraction-free)), and
improves the dependence of the cost on the dimension.

## Floating-point LLL: the L² algorithm {#l2}

The Gram–Schmidt data need not be exact: it suffices to know them well
enough to decide the size reductions and Lovász' conditions. Schnorr and
Euchner [@SE1994] introduced floating-point LLL; Nguyen and Stehlé
[@NS2005; @NS2009] gave the first provable variant, L², whose bit
complexity is quadratic in $\log B$, by computing the Gram–Schmidt
coefficients from the exact Gram matrix entries $\langle b_i, b_j
\rangle$ with a Cholesky-like recurrence in floating point:
$$
r_{kj} = \langle b_k, b_j \rangle - \sum_{l < j} \mu_{jl} r_{kl}, \qquad
\mu_{kj} = r_{kj} / r_{jj},
$$
and by using *lazy size reduction*: the size reduction of $b_k$ is
repeated, recomputing $r_{kj}$ and $\mu_{kj}$ from the updated Gram
entries, until all $|\mu_{kj}| \le \bar\eta$ for a slightly smaller
$\bar\eta$. They prove that a precision of $d \log_2 \frac{(1 + \eta)^2}
{\delta - \eta^2} + o(d)$ bits, that is about $1.6 d$ bits for parameters
close to $(1, \frac12)$, suffices. This is `fmpz_lll`'s algorithm; see
also the survey [@Stehle2010] and the implementation notes in
[@Ste2005].

::: algorithm L² main loop, as in fmpz_lll {#alg-l2}
**Input:** a basis $B$ (or its Gram matrix); $\delta$, $\eta$.

1. $\bar\delta \gets (\delta + 1)/2$, $\bar\eta \gets (\eta + \frac12)/2$;
   skip leading zero vectors; $\kappa \gets$ the index after the first
   nonzero vector.
2. While $\kappa < d$:
   1. *Babai step*: size-reduce $b_\kappa$ against $b_0, \ldots,
      b_{\kappa - 1}$ lazily until $|\mu_{\kappa j}| \le \bar\eta$; compute
      $s_i = \lVert b_\kappa \rVert^2 - \sum_{j < i} \mu_{\kappa j}
      r_{\kappa j}$, the squared norm of the projection of $b_\kappa$
      orthogonal to $b_0, \ldots, b_{i-1}$, for all $i \le \kappa$.
   2. *Lovász test*: if $\bar\delta r_{\kappa-1,\kappa-1} \le
      s_{\kappa-1}$, set $r_{\kappa\kappa} \gets s_\kappa$ and
      $\kappa \gets \kappa + 1$.
   3. *Insertion*: otherwise let $\kappa' < \kappa$ be the largest index
      with $\bar\delta r_{\kappa'-1,\kappa'-1} < s_{\kappa'-1}$, or the
      first position after the zero vectors if there is none. This is the
      position to which $b_\kappa$ would move by successive swaps. Move
      $b_\kappa$ there, shifting the vectors in between, update the
      Gram–Schmidt data and the Gram matrix, and set
      $\kappa \gets \kappa' + 1$. If the moved vector is zero (linearly
      dependent input), it becomes one more leading zero vector.
:::

The test with $\bar\delta > \delta$ and $\bar\eta < \eta$ leaves a margin
for the rounding errors, so that the output is $(\delta,
\eta)$-reduced when the precision is sufficient. If the Babai step does
not make progress, that is, if after 20 iterations the largest exponent
of the $\mu_{\kappa j}$ fails to decrease by 5, the precision is deemed
insufficient and the function returns $-1$.

### Precision strategies {#precision}

`fmpz_lll` implements the L² loop with three kinds of arithmetic and
moves to the next one when the previous one fails or does not produce a
reduced basis:

- `fmpz_lll_d` works in `double`. Each basis vector is approximated by a
  vector of doubles scaled by a power of two (`_fmpz_vec_get_d_vec_2exp`),
  so that the exponent range of doubles is not a limitation, and the
  Gram matrix entries are computed from these approximations (gram type
  `APPROX`) or kept exactly as `fmpz` (gram type `EXACT`). After 50
  failures of the fast Babai step it switches to the heuristic one for one
  iteration.
- `fmpz_lll_d_heuristic` computes the approximate dot products with
  `fmpz_lll_heuristic_dot`, which detects cancellation (a result much
  smaller than the product of the norms) and then recomputes the dot
  product exactly from the integer vectors, an idea from fplll
  [@Ste2005; @Stehle2010].
- `fmpz_lll_mpf2` works with floating-point numbers of a given
  precision, using `nfloat` when the precision allows it and `arf`
  otherwise; when the entries are small, the Gram entries are computed
  exactly from a packed copy of the basis and then rounded.
  `fmpz_lll_mpf` calls it with precisions $64, 128, \ldots, 1280$ bits and
  then doubles, until the result is reduced.

`fmpz_lll_wrapper` runs `fmpz_lll_d`; if it fails or the result is not
certified reduced, `fmpz_lll_d_heuristic` (for bases with approximate
Gram matrix); and finally `fmpz_lll_mpf`. For a basis given by its Gram
matrix, or with an exact Gram matrix, it goes directly from `fmpz_lll_d`
to `fmpz_lll_mpf`.

### Packed bases {#packed}

When the entries are small, the floating-point variants keep the basis in
a packed form (`fmpz_lll_packed_t`): every entry occupies the same number
of limbs in two's complement, and the rows are contiguous. Row operations
$b_k \gets b_k - x b_j$ are then done limb-wise modulo a power of two,
which is exact because every row carries an upper bound on the bit length
of its entries that all operations maintain; the number of limbs per
entry grows and shrinks as needed. This is much cheaper than operating on
`fmpz` entries, and the dot products for exact Gram entries use
fixed-size kernels.

### Certified reducedness {#certify}

After the floating-point algorithm, `fmpz_lll_is_reduced` checks that the
output really is reduced. `fmpz_lll_is_reduced_d` follows Villard
[@Villard2007]: it computes an approximate QR factorisation $A \approx QR$
of the basis in double precision, an approximate inverse $V \approx
R^{-1}$, and with directed rounding (`fesetround`) rigorous enclosures of
$RV - I$ and of the backward error, from which it derives intervals
containing the true Gram–Schmidt quantities and checks the LLL conditions
on them. `fmpz_lll_is_reduced_mpfr` does the same with `nfloat` numbers of
the given precision and directed rounding. Both are one-sided: a return
value of 1 is a proof, 0 is inconclusive. `fmpz_lll_is_reduced` falls back
to the exact tests `fmpz_mat_is_reduced` (ball arithmetic, then rational
arithmetic) and `fmpz_mat_is_reduced_gram`.

## Removals {#removals}

In [van Hoeij's factoring algorithm](algorithms/polynomials/factoring-integers#van-hoeij)
[@vHo2002; @vHN2010], only the lattice
vectors of norm at most a bound are of interest, and vectors with large
Gram–Schmidt norm at the end of a reduced basis can be discarded. The
functions with removal take a bound `gs_B` and return the number `newd` of
vectors to keep: after the reduction, the last vectors whose squared
Gram–Schmidt norm exceeds the bound (by a factor 2 in the floating-point
estimate) are dropped from the count, and the first `newd` vectors are
$(\delta, \eta)$-reduced. A vector of a reduced basis whose Gram–Schmidt
norm exceeds the bound cannot contribute to a vector of norm at most the
bound with the following vectors. During the reduction, the
dimension is decreased as soon as the last vector is estimated to exceed
the bound. The knapsack variant `fmpz_lll_d_with_removal_knapsack`
additionally size-reduces each new vector in advance the first time it is
reached, which pays off for the knapsack-type lattices of factoring.

## Truncation: ULLL {#ulll}

The cost of floating-point LLL grows with the size of the entries, while
the transformation found by reducing a truncated basis, with only the
leading $s$ bits of each entry, often does most of the work. `fmpz_lll`
uses this strategy, called ULLL in FLINT and developed by Novocin and van
Hoeij [@Nov2008; @vHN2010] for factoring:

::: algorithm ULLL {#alg-ulll}
**Input:** a basis $B$ ($d \times n$) with $b$-bit entries; a size $s$
(FLINT uses $s = 250$).

1. While $b > s$:
   1. form the $d \times (d + n)$ matrix $(I_d \mid \lfloor B / 2^{b-s}
      \rfloor)$ and reduce it with `fmpz_lll_wrapper_with_removal_knapsack`;
   2. let $U$ be its first $d$ columns, a unimodular transformation; if
      $U = I$, stop truncating; otherwise $B \gets UB$;
   3. if the size of $B$ has decreased by at least $s/4$ bits, repeat with
      the new $b$; otherwise stop truncating.
2. Reduce $B$ itself with `fmpz_lll_wrapper_with_removal_knapsack`.
:::

Since each pass only applies unimodular transformations, the lattice is
unchanged and the final pass guarantees reducedness; the truncated passes
work with entries of $s + O(d)$ bits, mostly in doubles. For Gram matrix
input there is no truncation. `fmpz_lll_storjohann_ulll` is the same
strategy with `fmpz_mat_lll_storjohann`.
